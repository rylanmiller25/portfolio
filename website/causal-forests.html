Causal Forests

An A/B test tells you what a change does on average. A causal forest goes further and tells you the part that actually drives strategy - who responds, who doesn't, and by how much.

Imagine running an experiment where you offer a discount to a random half of your customers and watch what happens. The classic result is a single number: on average, the discount lifted sign-ups by, say, five points. That average is real, but it hides everything interesting. Some customers were always going to buy and the discount just gave away margin. Others were genuinely won over. A third group didn't budge at all. The average quietly blends all of them together.

This project builds the tool that pulls those groups apart. It applies causal forests - a machine learning method designed specifically for experiments - to estimate how much each individual customer responds to a treatment, rather than settling for one population-wide average.

Why heterogeneous effects matter

"Heterogeneous treatment effects" is the technical name for a simple business reality: the same offer lands differently on different people. The average effect from an A/B test answers "should we do this at all?" It does not answer the more valuable question - "who should we do it for?"

That second question is where the money is. If a discount strongly moves one segment and does nothing for another, you can stop spending on the customers who were always going to convert and concentrate the offer where it actually changes minds. Causal forests give you a per-customer estimate of the effect - what researchers call a Conditional Average Treatment Effect, or CATE - so targeting decisions rest on evidence instead of hunches.

How it works

The hard part of measuring per-person effects is that you can never see both outcomes for the same customer - they either got the discount or they didn't. A causal forest works around this by growing thousands of decision trees that repeatedly split customers into similar groups, then comparing treated and untreated people within each group. Averaging over the whole forest produces a smooth, personalized estimate for everyone.

The forest is built with a technique called honesty: each tree uses one slice of the data to decide where to split and a completely separate slice to estimate the effect inside each split. That separation keeps the model from fooling itself into seeing patterns that aren't there, which is exactly the discipline you want before acting on the results. The implementation uses the well-established generalized random forest (GRF) framework in R, with two thousand honest trees.

To prove the whole pipeline is trustworthy, the project runs in simulation mode. It generates a synthetic experiment - ten thousand enterprise customers offered a seat discount - where the true effect for every customer is known in advance, baked in to vary by geography, company size, pricing tier, and how long they've been a customer. Because the right answer is known, every estimate can be checked against ground truth. The forest never sees the true effects; it only sees the simulated experiment, just as it would with real data.

Before trusting any estimate, the project runs the full suite of standard GRF diagnostics: an overlap check that every customer really could have landed in either group, a balance check that the two groups are comparable, a calibration test that the model's predictions line up with reality, and a targeting test that measures whether acting on the model beats acting at random.

What the results showed

The pipeline passed every check. The model's estimate of the overall average effect was nearly perfect, landing right on the true value. It also detected genuine differences between customers - real heterogeneity, not noise - and pointed in the correct direction, though like all forests it compresses the spread a little, making the extremes look slightly tamer than they are.

Most importantly, the model recovered the true structure that was simulated into the data. When you break the predicted effects down by customer segment, the pattern matches the design: the largest responses come from bigger, newer accounts on mid-tier plans - the customers with the most room to grow - while small businesses, long-tenured customers, and those already on the top tier barely move.

Knowing customers differ only pays off if you can act on the ranking. The targeting test below confirms that focusing the discount on the customers the model rates highest beats handing it out at random. The advantage is real but moderate - and when an offer carries a real cost, even a moderate improvement in who receives it can move the return.

Takeaways

  • Recovered the true average effect almost exactly, confirming the estimates are well-calibrated.
  • Detected genuine, correctly-ordered differences between customer segments.
  • Reproduced the simulated structure - effects rise with company size and fall with tenure.
  • Showed that targeting the model's top picks beats a random rollout.
